Arabic Transliteration, Methodology, and Harakat Model Summary

1. Importance of Arabic Transliteration

Arabic transliteration is an important natural language processing problem because Arabic users routinely move between multiple writing systems, language varieties, and communicative settings. In formal contexts, Arabic is written in Arabic script; however, in digital communication, many users write Arabic dialects using Latin letters and numerals, a practice commonly called Arabizi, Arabic chat alphabet, ASCII-ized Arabic, or Franco-Arabic [1], [2]. This practice emerged partly from historical technical constraints, including keyboards, encodings, and messaging systems that did not always support Arabic script, but it persists for social, stylistic, and dialectal reasons even when Arabic script is available [1].

The computational importance of Arabizi follows from three related facts. First, Arabizi is not a single standardized orthography. The same Arabic word may have several Latin-script spellings, and numerals may represent Arabic phonemes absent from English orthography, such as 3 for ع or 7 for ح. Second, Arabizi often represents dialectal Arabic rather than Modern Standard Arabic. Dialectal Arabic is highly variable across regions and often lacks prescriptive spelling standards, which complicates supervised learning and evaluation [3], [4]. Third, most Arabic NLP tools assume Arabic script input. Search, language identification, machine translation, speech applications, lexicon lookup, sentiment analysis, and educational systems can therefore fail or degrade when user content appears in Arabizi [5], [6].

The task also matters from an access and preservation perspective. Dialectal Arabic is often the language of everyday communication, humor, family interaction, music, oral history, and social media commentary. When dialectal text is represented in Arabizi, it becomes less accessible to readers, researchers, and tools that expect Arabic script. A reliable transliteration system can therefore serve as a bridge between informal user-generated content and downstream Arabic NLP pipelines. For Palestinian Arabic specifically, transliteration also supports dialect documentation: resources such as Maknuune encode lexical knowledge that is valuable not only for machine learning but also for preserving culturally specific vocabulary, pronunciation, and usage [3].

Arabic transliteration is distinct from translation. Translation changes language; transliteration changes writing system while attempting to preserve the underlying lexical item or pronunciation. However, Arabizi-to-Arabic transliteration is harder than many formal transliteration tasks because the source text is informal, ambiguous, and dialectal. A model may need to infer whether "7aki" corresponds to حكي, حَكِي, or another dialectally appropriate form, and it may need to tolerate inconsistent vowel spelling, repeated letters, and English-influenced conventions. These properties make the task a strong fit for deep learning: rather than manually writing all possible rules, a neural model can learn statistical correspondences from aligned examples while still producing ranked alternatives when uncertainty remains.

For a deep learning class, Arabizi transliteration is especially instructive because it is a low-resource sequence transduction problem. The mapping is neither a simple character substitution nor a full semantic translation task. It requires the model to learn phonological correspondences, spelling conventions, dialectal variation, and ambiguity. For example, a Latin sequence may correspond to multiple Arabic-script forms, and Arabic script forms without diacritics may correspond to multiple pronunciations. A useful system should therefore not only return one answer, but also expose ranked alternatives with probabilities.

2. Literature Review of Previous Transliteration Attempts

Early work on Arabic chat alphabet focused on describing the sociolinguistic phenomenon. Palfreyman and Al Khalil studied ASCII-ized Gulf Arabic in instant messaging and showed that the writing system reflects a combination of computer constraints, Arabic script conventions, English orthography, spoken dialects, and user creativity [1]. This observation is central for modeling: Arabizi is not merely noisy text, but a socially embedded orthographic system with patterned variability.

Computational approaches initially treated Arabizi conversion with feature engineering, transliteration mining, finite-state methods, and language modeling. Darwish addressed both Arabizi detection and conversion, using word- and sequence-level features for identifying Arabizi mixed with English and transliteration mining with language modeling for conversion to Arabic script. That system achieved 98.5% identification accuracy and 88.7% conversion accuracy, while noting that many errors reflected spelling and morphological variants rather than completely wrong conversions [5]. Elbadrashiny et al. framed Romanized dialectal Arabic conversion as mapping Arabizi to Arabic script under the CODA convention. Their system used character-level finite-state transducers to generate possible transliterations and a dialectal Arabic morphological analyzer to filter candidates [6]. These systems established important baselines, but they depended on engineered resources and external analyzers.

Neural sequence-to-sequence modeling changed the transliteration landscape. Sutskever et al. demonstrated that encoder-decoder neural networks can learn general sequence mappings [7], and Bahdanau et al. improved this framework with attention, allowing the decoder to focus on relevant parts of the source sequence [8]. Rosca and Breuel applied sequence-to-sequence neural models directly to transliteration and showed that neural approaches could achieve strong results on transliteration datasets [9]. For Arabizi specifically, Talafha, Abuammar, and Al-Ayyoub introduced Atar, an attention-based LSTM encoder-decoder model for Jordanian Arabizi-to-Arabic transliteration, along with a parallel corpus of more than 25,000 pairs. Their system reported 79% accuracy and a BLEU score of 88.49 [10].

The transition from finite-state and language-modeling pipelines to neural encoder-decoder systems is important methodologically. Earlier systems often decomposed the problem into candidate generation, filtering, and ranking. This has the advantage of interpretability and can incorporate linguistic constraints, but it requires carefully designed components. Neural systems instead learn the mapping end-to-end from examples. In transliteration, where the alignment between source and target is mostly monotonic but not perfectly one-to-one, attention-based models are particularly appropriate. They can learn that one Arabizi character may map to one Arabic character, that a digit may map to a consonant absent from Latin orthography, or that a vowel in Arabizi may correspond to a haraka rather than an Arabic letter.

More recent work has moved toward Transformer architectures. Vaswani et al. introduced the Transformer, replacing recurrent computation with self-attention and enabling stronger parallel sequence modeling [11]. This architecture is well suited to transliteration because each output character can attend to the full input string rather than only to a compressed final recurrent state. Hajbi et al. presented a Transformer-based Moroccan Arabizi-to-Arabic transliteration model trained on more than 33,000 word pairs, reporting approximately 92.77% word accuracy and 2.38% character error rate on unseen data [12]. This work is closely aligned with the present project because both use character-level modeling and Transformer sequence transduction to address low-resource dialectal transliteration.

The data resource used in this project, Maknuune, is also significant. Dibas et al. introduced Maknuune as a large open Palestinian Arabic lexicon with more than 36,000 entries, 17,000 lemmas, and 3,700 roots. The entries include diacritized Arabic orthography, phonological transcription, English glosses, and additional lexical information [3]. This makes Maknuune unusually valuable for transliteration because it connects dialectal lexical items to Arabic orthography and pronunciation-related information.

Maknuune is especially suitable for this project because it supports both script conversion and pronunciation-aware modeling. Many generic Arabic resources focus on Modern Standard Arabic, but Arabizi often represents spoken dialect. A Palestinian Arabic lexicon therefore gives the model examples that better match the intended domain. In addition, the presence of diacritized Arabic orthography allows the project to move beyond undiacritized script. This is important because Arabic letters alone do not fully specify short vowels, while Arabizi spellings frequently do. The dataset therefore supports a methodology in which diacritization is not merely an auxiliary task, but a bridge between Arabic orthography and Romanized pronunciation.

Arabic diacritization is another relevant line of work because Arabic script is often written without short vowels. Automatic diacritization has been studied using rule-based, statistical, and neural approaches. Azmi and Almajed surveyed automatic Arabic diacritization techniques and emphasized the difficulty of resolving ambiguity in undiacritized Arabic [13]. Rashwan et al. proposed a stochastic Arabic diacritizer using factorized and unfactorized textual features [14]. Later systems adopted deep learning, including feature-rich BiLSTM models [15], BERT-based syntactic diacritic restoration [16], Transformer-based diacritization [17], and robust BERT-based systems such as SukounBERT.v2 [18]. These studies motivate the harakat component of the current project: reconstructing missing diacritics can provide pronunciation-sensitive intermediate structure for Arabic-to-Arabizi prediction.

3. Methodology

This project treats transliteration as a pair of supervised sequence-to-sequence learning problems: Arabizi-to-Arabic and Arabic-to-Arabizi. The Arabizi-to-Arabic direction maps Latin-script dialectal inputs to Arabic script. This is modeled using a Transformer architecture because Transformers are effective for sequence transduction, support parallel training, and use attention to model long-range dependencies between source and target characters [11]. The model outputs ranked candidate sequences using beam search. Rather than treating the top candidate as the only useful output, beam search preserves multiple plausible hypotheses. A softmax threshold or beam threshold can be used to retain candidates accounting for a specified share of probability mass. In practical terms, this produces a list of most likely alternatives; lower-ranked retained alternatives can also be interpreted as less likely but still plausible candidates within the model's uncertainty region.

The use of beam search is central because transliteration is inherently ambiguous. A greedy decoder that selects the most likely next character at each time step can produce a locally plausible but globally suboptimal sequence. Beam search instead keeps several partial hypotheses alive, allowing the model to recover if a lower-probability early character leads to a better full-word prediction. The project extends this idea with threshold-based prediction: at each decoding step, the model examines the softmax distribution and expands enough alternatives to cover a desired probability mass. This is useful for a presentation because it makes uncertainty visible. Instead of presenting the neural network as if it always "knows" one answer, the system can show a ranked set of alternatives and their relative likelihood.

The Arabic-to-Arabizi direction is more challenging because Arabic script often omits harakat. Without short vowels, the Arabic source under-specifies pronunciation, while Arabizi often encodes pronunciation more directly. To address this, the project introduces a harakat reconstruction step. First, a harakat_adder model predicts a diacritized version of the Arabic input. Then the reconstructed diacritized form can be used as a richer intermediate representation for predicting Arabizi. Methodologically, this is a form of intermediate representation learning: the system decomposes Arabic-to-Arabizi conversion into (1) Arabic without harakat to Arabic with harakat, and (2) pronunciation-informed Arabic to Arabizi. This is linguistically motivated because harakat encode short vowels and other phonological distinctions that are important for Romanized spelling.

This design also reflects an error-analysis hypothesis. If Arabic-to-Arabizi prediction is attempted directly from undiacritized Arabic, the model must simultaneously infer vowels, pronunciation, and Romanization conventions. By inserting the harakat_adder model, the pipeline separates these concerns. The first model reconstructs likely vowelization; the second model can then learn a more transparent mapping from a pronunciation-rich Arabic representation to Arabizi. This does not eliminate ambiguity, because harakat prediction itself is imperfect, but it gives the second stage a more informative input than bare Arabic script. The approach is therefore comparable to using a latent linguistic annotation layer in a larger neural pipeline.

The prediction workflow is also designed to support uncertainty analysis. The harakat prediction notebook loads the trained harakat_adder.pt model, iterates through all arabic_stripped observations, runs a 90% softmax-threshold beam search, saves a JSON-like list of candidate predictions, and periodically writes progress to maknuune-v1.0.1_cleaned_predictions.csv. This checkpointed workflow is important because full-dataset beam search is computationally expensive; periodic saving allows interrupted Colab sessions to resume without recomputing completed rows.

The methodology emphasizes reproducibility and operational practicality. The training notebooks save model bundles with configuration, vocabularies, and weights, so inference can reconstruct the exact architecture used during training. Metrics are saved to CSV files, making the experiments inspectable outside the notebook. Long-running prediction scripts update their output file periodically, which is particularly important in Google Colab where sessions can disconnect. These engineering decisions are part of the research method: they make it possible to rerun experiments, inspect intermediate outputs, and compare model behavior across decoding strategies.

4. Harakat Model Architecture and Metrics

The harakat model is a character-level encoder-decoder Transformer. Its input is arabic_stripped, and its target is arabic_harakat. The cleaned local dataset contains 36,302 rows with columns for Arabizi, diacritized Arabic, and stripped Arabic. The data are shuffled with a fixed seed and split into approximately 80% training, 10% validation, and 10% testing. Character vocabularies are built from the training split, with special tokens for padding, beginning of sequence, end of sequence, and unknown characters.

Architecturally, the model uses learned source and target embeddings with dimensionality d_model = 192, sinusoidal positional encoding, 6 attention heads, 4 encoder layers, 4 decoder layers, a feedforward dimension of 768, and dropout of 0.15. The decoder uses causal masking so that it cannot look ahead during training. Optimization uses AdamW, weight decay, gradient clipping, and a OneCycle learning-rate schedule. The saved model bundle includes the state dictionary, source and target vocabularies, model configuration, and best validation metrics.

The model is trained at the character level rather than the word-piece or word level. This choice is appropriate for a lexicon-sized dataset because character vocabularies are small, robust to rare words, and naturally aligned with transliteration and diacritization. A word-level model would suffer from sparse vocabulary coverage, while a subword model might obscure the fine-grained relationship between letters and harakat. Character-level prediction also makes exact-sequence evaluation meaningful: the output is correct only when every predicted letter and mark matches the target.

The principal metric is exact sequence accuracy. This is a strict metric: a prediction is counted as correct only if the entire predicted harakat sequence exactly matches the target sequence after ignoring padding. The metrics file records 90 epochs. The best validation exact-sequence accuracy occurred at epoch 83, reaching 0.4983, or approximately 49.83%. The best validation loss occurred at epoch 80, with cross-entropy loss of approximately 0.2662. At epoch 90, the model remained close to its best validation performance, with validation exact-sequence accuracy of approximately 49.59% and training exact-sequence accuracy of approximately 56.27%.

Length-based metrics show that performance is strongly affected by sequence length. Near the best validation epoch, the model achieved approximately 48.15% accuracy for 0-9 character sequences and 57.10% for 10-19 character sequences, while accuracy was substantially lower for longer sequences. This pattern is expected because exact-sequence accuracy compounds errors: every additional character creates another opportunity for the sequence to fail the all-or-nothing match criterion. Therefore, the harakat model should be interpreted as a useful pronunciation-reconstruction component, especially for short and medium lexical items, while longer sequences may require additional context, larger training data, or a less brittle evaluation metric such as character error rate.

The gap between training and validation sequence accuracy is moderate rather than extreme, suggesting that the model is learning generalizable correspondences rather than simply memorizing the training set. At the same time, the validation accuracy near 50% indicates that the task remains difficult under exact-match scoring. This is unsurprising for dialectal lexical data: multiple vocalizations may be plausible, and a single-character diacritic error marks the whole sequence incorrect. For future evaluation, exact sequence accuracy should be complemented with character-level accuracy, diacritic error rate, top-k accuracy from beam search, and human review of near-miss predictions. These additional metrics would better distinguish severe errors from minor alternatives.

Overall, the system combines modern neural sequence transduction with linguistically motivated preprocessing. The Arabizi-to-Arabic component addresses noisy dialectal Romanization through Transformer-based prediction and beam search. The Arabic-to-Arabizi component uses automatic harakat reconstruction to restore phonological information before predicting Romanized output. This architecture reflects a central deep learning principle: when the raw input omits important latent structure, an auxiliary model can reconstruct an intermediate representation that makes the final prediction problem better conditioned.
